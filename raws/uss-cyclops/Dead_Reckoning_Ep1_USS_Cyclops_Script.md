# DEAD RECKONING — Episode 1
## "USS Cyclops — 306 People Vanished. What Killed Her?"

**Runtime target:** ~20 minutes
**Format:** Hook → Case → Theory 1 (War) → Theory 2 (Sabotage) → Theory 3 (Physics) → Verdict → Open Question
**Evidence tags used on screen:** CONFIRMED / PROBABLE / POSSIBLE / UNSUPPORTED / DISPROVEN
**Case status at close:** PROBABLE (~75%)

Bracketed lines are visual/on-screen directions, not spoken. Everything else is teleprompter text.

---

### [0:00–2:00] THE LAST KNOWN POSITION

[ON SCREEN: Evidence board opens — KNOWN panel populating]

On March 4th, 1918, a US Navy ship called the Cyclops left port in Barbados. She was 542 feet long, carrying over 300 people, and headed for Baltimore.

She never arrived.

No distress call. No wreckage. No lifeboats. No oil slick. Nothing. To this day, she's still the single largest loss of life in US naval history that wasn't caused by combat. 306 people, gone, without a trace.

For over a hundred years, the easy answer has been the Bermuda Triangle. Sea monsters, portals, whatever you want to believe in. We're not doing that here.

This is Dead Reckoning, and every case we cover gets the same treatment — we lay out what's actually documented, we grade the evidence, and we close with a verdict, even when that verdict is "we don't know." Today the case is the Cyclops, and the question isn't "what mysterious force took her." It's simpler and, honestly, more disturbing than that: was this ship taken by the war she was sailing through, or was she killed by the ship herself?

Let's open the file.

---

### [2:00–6:00] THEORY 1 — THE WAR

[ON SCREEN: KNOWN panel — "WWI active. Cargo: manganese ore, used in armor plate and artillery steel."]

Start with the obvious theory, because it's the one the Navy itself suspected in 1918. This is the middle of World War 1. German submarines are actively hunting Allied shipping in the Atlantic. The Cyclops was carrying 10,800 tons of manganese ore — a material that was critical for making armor-grade steel. If you're Germany, that's a target worth sinking.

So: did a U-boat get her?

[ON SCREEN: Evidence tag — WAR THEORY: under review]

Here's where you have to go past the folklore and into the actual German naval records — the war diaries kept by the German Submarine Command, the Befehlshaber der Unterseeboote. These logs survived the war and were reviewed afterward by naval historians on both sides.

And they say something very specific: in March of 1918, Germany's long-range submarines — the ones capable of crossing the Atlantic — simply were not in the area. The first German U-boat to reach American waters that year was U-151, and it didn't arrive off the Virginia coast until May 15th, more than two months after the Cyclops vanished. The official US Navy postwar review of German submarine activity confirmed that zero German submarines operated west of the 40th meridian during March 1918. Not "we don't know." Zero.

No German commander ever claimed the kill either — and U-boat commanders reported their sinkings. A collier of this size, lost that dramatically, would have been a story worth telling. Nobody told it, because nobody was there to sink her.

[ON SCREEN: Evidence tag update — WAR THEORY: DISPROVEN]

You could also ask about mines. Same answer. There were no minefields laid along that open-ocean corridor between the Caribbean and the Chesapeake — mines were a coastal weapon, not something you'd find in deep water on a transatlantic shipping lane. And a mine or torpedo strike leaves a signature: floating debris, an oil slick, wreckage, something. None of that was ever found.

So the theory that made the news in 1918 — enemy action — doesn't survive contact with the actual German records. That's strike one, and it's a clean one. The war was the backdrop to this story. It wasn't the cause.

Which means we have to ask an uglier question. If it wasn't the enemy... was it someone on board?

---

### [6:00–10:00] THEORY 2 — SABOTAGE, MUTINY, AND THE CAPTAIN

[ON SCREEN: KNOWN panel — "Commanding officer: Lt. Cmdr. George W. Worley"]

This is where the story gets personal, and where a lot of channels just repeat the gossip without checking it. So let's check it.

The Cyclops was commanded by Lieutenant Commander George W. Worley. And Worley had a secret — though "secret" might be generous, because a lot of people seemed to know it. He wasn't born George Worley. He was born Johann Friedrich Wichmann, in Germany, and he'd jumped ship as a young man in San Francisco back in the 1870s, taken an American name, and built a career as a US merchant mariner before getting a wartime naval commission in 1917.

To a crew serving during World War 1 against Germany, a German-born commanding officer was, understandably, a source of suspicion. Contemporary accounts describe crew referring to him as "that damned Dutchman." That's documented. It's not invented.

[ON SCREEN: On-screen segment — "POPULAR STORY vs. DOCUMENTED"]

Here's where we run our "popular story versus documented" check, because this is exactly the kind of detail that gets exaggerated with every retelling.

**Popular story:** Worley was a German spy, secretly working to deliver the ship, her war-critical cargo, and a group of naval prisoners straight to Germany.

**Documented:** Naval intelligence — the Office of Naval Intelligence, specifically — investigated this after the disappearance. They found no financial connections, no diplomatic contact, no evidence tying Worley to German intelligence in any way. His name change was common practice for foreign-born sailors trying to get an American command license, not a cover story. And logistically, the theory doesn't even hold up — a ship "delivered" to the enemy needs a destination. No trace of the Cyclops, her cargo, or her crew ever turned up in a German port, in a neutral country, or in any postwar German asset inventory. Nobody was ever held prisoner. There's no delivery, because there was nowhere for the ship to go.

**Status: unsupported. Formally investigated and cleared.**

[ON SCREEN: Evidence tag — SABOTAGE / TREASON: UNSUPPORTED]

Now, was Worley a good commanding officer? That's a separate question, and here the record is a lot less flattering. A petition signed by around 40 crew members in 1917 describes him as, in their words, unfit for command — accounts describe erratic, sometimes drunken behavior, including one report of him chasing a junior officer across the ship with a pistol. One officer who transferred off before the final voyage described him as gruff, eccentric, and an indifferent seaman. There was also a passenger on that final voyage — the US Consul-General to Rio, a man named Gottschalk — who had his own rumors of pro-German sympathy attached to him. Those rumors, too, were investigated and dropped; Gottschalk was actually heading home specifically to volunteer for US Army combat duty, to put the rumors to rest.

So here's the honest read on Theory 2. The spy story: unsupported, formally cleared by naval intelligence at the time. But the competence question is real, and it matters — not because Worley betrayed his country, but because of what an erratic, disliked, possibly impaired commanding officer means for how carefully a ship gets loaded, inspected, and handled before she sails into a war zone.

Which is the perfect bridge into the theory that actually holds up.

---

### [10:00–15:00] THEORY 3 — THE PHYSICS OF THE SHIP

[ON SCREEN: KNOWN panel updates — "Cargo: manganese ore. Engines: 1 of 2 operational. Departure draft: Plimsoll line submerged."]

If it wasn't the enemy, and it wasn't treason, we're left with the ship herself. And once you start pulling the technical record, you find that the Cyclops left Barbados carrying three separate, compounding problems — any one of which would be concerning on its own. Together, they're close to a formula for disaster.

**Problem one: the engine.**

The Cyclops had two engines. By the time she reached Brazil, one of them — the starboard engine — had a fractured cylinder and was completely out of commission. A Navy survey board in Rio actually reviewed this and made a call: rather than delay wartime cargo delivery for repairs, they authorized her to cross the open Atlantic on one engine, deferring the fix until she reached Baltimore. That cut her top speed roughly in half, down to somewhere around 8 to 10 knots. But speed isn't even the real issue — a ship built for two engines, running on one, doesn't track straight. It creates a constant sideways pull, which means the helmsman has to fight the wheel just to hold a course. In calm water that's an inconvenience. In a storm, it means the ship can lose the ability to point her bow into incoming waves — which is exactly the position you don't want to be caught in.

**Problem two: the cargo, and this is the part almost nobody explains properly.**

The Cyclops was designed to carry coal. Coal is bulky but light — it fills a ship's hold from bottom to top, spreading weight across the whole height of the vessel and keeping her balanced in a way that absorbs rolling motion smoothly.

Manganese ore is nothing like coal. It's roughly five times denser. So when they loaded 10,800 tons of it into her holds, that cargo weighed as much as a full load of coal — but it only filled about a third of the available space, sitting in low, compact piles right at the bottom of the ship.

[ON SCREEN: Simple animated comparison — coal filling the hold vs. ore sitting low and dense]

That changes the ship's center of gravity dramatically. It makes her what naval architects call "stiff" — instead of rolling gently with the waves, a stiff ship snaps back upright violently, fast, almost jerkily. That sounds like it should be a good thing — stable, right? It isn't. Those violent snap-rolls put enormous, repeated stress on the hull and on the cargo itself. And because the ore had been loaded quickly, in loose piles, with no dividing boards to hold it in place, that kind of violent rolling could cause tons of ore to physically shift to one side. Add in seawater working through the hatch covers — which, on this ship, were just wooden boards and canvas tarps, not sealed steel — and you get moisture soaking into the ore from below. Enough moisture turns dense ore into something closer to a slurry, which shifts even more easily and doesn't shift back.

**Problem three: she was overloaded, and there are eyewitnesses to prove it.**

When the Cyclops left Barbados, people onshore — harbor officials, the US Consul — reported that her Plimsoll line, the marker painted on a ship's hull that shows the maximum safe loading depth, was completely underwater. That's not a subtle warning sign. That's a ship sitting lower in the water than she was ever designed to sit, with almost no reserve buoyancy left if seas got rough.

[ON SCREEN: Evidence tag — OVERLOADING: CONFIRMED, eyewitness accounts]

And seas did get rough. US Weather Bureau records for March 9th and 10th, 1918, confirm a severe storm — sustained gale-force winds, somewhere around 35 to 50 knots — moving through exactly the stretch of ocean the Cyclops would have been crossing at her reduced, one-engine speed. We even have a secondhand data point: another ship, the SS Amolco, was caught in that same storm nearby and nearly didn't survive it. Her captain later said, on the record, that if he'd been carrying manganese ore instead of molasses, he doesn't think his ship would have made it.

So picture the full stack: a ship running on half her engine power, unable to hold her bow steady into the waves, sitting dangerously low in the water, carrying a cargo that made her snap-roll violently instead of ride smoothly — and then she sails directly into a Force 8 to 10 gale.

[ON SCREEN: Evidence tag — PHYSICAL FAILURE THEORY: PROBABLE]

There's one more piece of evidence, and it might be the most convincing one in the whole case. The Cyclops had two sister ships, built to the identical design — the USS Proteus and the USS Nereus. In late 1941, more than twenty years later, both of those ships were sailing the same general route, carrying a different but similarly dense cargo — bauxite ore, used for aluminum. Both vanished. No distress call. No wreckage. Within three weeks of each other. Postwar review of German and Italian submarine records confirmed neither ship was attacked by enemy submarines either.

Three ships. Same design. Same failure signature. Two different wars, two different dense cargoes, over twenty years apart. That's not a coincidence you can wave away — that's a pattern, and patterns are exactly the kind of evidence a cold-case review is built to find.

---

### [15:00–18:00] THE VERDICT

[ON SCREEN: Evidence board — full theory list with final tags]

So let's close the file the way we always do — not with certainty we don't have, but with an honest reading of what the evidence actually supports.

The war theory: disproven. German naval records confirm no submarine was anywhere near her position.

The sabotage and treason theory: investigated at the time, and unsupported. Worley's German birth made him a convenient suspect, but there's no evidence he — or anyone else on board — deliberately caused this loss.

The physical failure theory — a combination of a crippled engine, an unstable, overloaded cargo of dense ore, and a direct hit from a major Atlantic storm — that's where the evidence actually points. Not one smoking gun, but four or five smaller pieces of hard evidence that all line up in the same direction, backed up by two sister ships that failed the exact same way decades later.

[ON SCREEN: CASE STATUS — PROBABLE, ~75%]

We're calling this one Probable, not Solved, for one honest reason: the wreck of the Cyclops has never been found. Without the physical ship, we can't say with total certainty whether she broke apart under the stress, or rolled over and went down in the storm. Both are physically plausible failure modes, and we genuinely don't know which one happened first. That gap is real, and we're not going to pretend it isn't.

What we can say is this: the Cyclops wasn't taken by an enemy, and she wasn't betrayed from within. Based on everything documented — the engine, the cargo, the overloading, the storm, and the pattern repeated by her sister ships — she was most likely brought down by her own compromised physical limits. Not murdered by the war she was sailing through. Killed by her own physics.

### [18:00–End] THE OPEN QUESTION

The Navy never found the Cyclops. She's still down there somewhere, on the floor of the Atlantic, and the ocean hasn't given up her secrets yet.

But the math of her final voyage tells a story, even without the wreck to confirm it. A cracked engine. An impossible cargo. A storm she wasn't built to survive.
