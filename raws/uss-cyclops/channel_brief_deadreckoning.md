# Channel Brief: Maritime Case Files

*Working title — alternatives to test: "The Ship Disappearance Files," "Open Water Investigations," "Lost at Sea"*

**The one-sentence promise:**
> We investigate the ships that disappeared — using the evidence they left behind.

---

## 1. What the channel is about

A maritime investigation channel. Each episode opens a case: a real, documented ship disappearance, ghost-ship mystery, or persistent maritime legend. We lay out the competing theories, weigh them against the actual evidence, and close with a confidence-rated verdict — including, honestly, when the verdict is that nobody knows.

The ships are the subject matter. The product is the method:

> **Every episode is a case. Every case has evidence. Every case gets a judgment.**

That's the same psychological engine as true crime, applied somewhere it hasn't been used as a channel identity yet — not a new hook, a proven one relocated into an open format×market cell.

---

## 2. How it's distinct, and why that matters

Most channels in this space fall into one of two traps:

- **Shock-value listicles** ("10 ghost ships that will haunt you") — high volume, no resolution, no reason to subscribe. This is the saturated format.
- **Generic ship-content channels** with no narrative identity (the closest real competitor, The Microcosm, covers disasters/incidents but has no investigative arc).

We're neither. We're a structured, evidence-graded review of real cases — closer to a cold-case review than a scary-story compilation, and closer to an investigative bureau than a documentary channel.

**Important calibration:** we are not conducting new investigations — no FOIA requests, no new witnesses, no forensic breakthroughs. We are doing rigorous **structured review** of existing primary and secondary sources, with a critical lens most channels in this space don't apply. The language we use publicly ("case files," "we investigate") should promise exactly that — sourcing rigor and honest evidence-weighing — and never imply we're surfacing information nobody's seen.

**Why this matters beyond "it's different":**

1. **Monetization protection that's actually real.** The 2026 policy risk isn't solved by nice visuals — it's solved by the intellectual work being visibly, legibly ours: research → source selection → interpretation → argument → reconstruction → conclusion. The visual system exists to make that chain visible, not to substitute for it.
2. **A reason to subscribe, not just watch once.** A recurring format with a real payoff structure (see Section 4) gives viewers a reason to come back for the next case.

---

## 3. Content taxonomy — the actual long-term asset

Two case types, same investigative framework:

- **SHIP CASES** — "What happened?" (USS Cyclops, MV Joyita)
- **LEGEND CASES** — "Did this story actually happen?" (Ourang Medan — investigate claim → source → earliest appearance → embellishment → physical plausibility → verdict)

This isn't a side note — it roughly doubles the channel's viable content pool and solves the biggest structural risk in the original concept: **running out of strong ship disappearances.** There are only 15–30 disappearances with enough documentation, competing theories, and reconstructable narrative to sustain a full episode. There are far more *legends* to fact-check.

**The brand is "maritime investigations" as a structure, not "ship disappearances" as a genre.** That's what lets the channel eventually expand into shipwrecks, failed expeditions, WWII naval mysteries, engineering failures, and navigation disasters — all under the same case/evidence/verdict framework — instead of hitting a wall around episode 15–20.

## 4. The format spec

### Fixed grammar, variable mechanic

Every episode follows the same skeleton:

**HOOK → CASE → TIMELINE → THEORIES → EVIDENCE → VERDICT**

But the *investigative mechanic* inside that skeleton changes per episode — this is deliberate. A channel that reuses the exact same beat-for-beat experience every time risks reading as templated/repetitive, which is its own monetization risk. Standardize the grammar, not the experience:

| Episode | Investigative mechanic |
|---|---|
| USS Cyclops | Disappearance reconstruction (route, weather, radio range) |
| MV Joyita | Timeline contradiction analysis |
| Ourang Medan | Source authentication ("did this story happen?") |
| Future cases | Forensic cargo analysis, navigation reconstruction, etc. |

### The evidence-rating system (the fix for the verdict problem)

Most maritime disappearances don't resolve the way true-crime cases do. If the channel promises a verdict every time, and mostly delivers "we don't know," the format teaches audiences to expect a payoff it can't consistently give. The fix: make the *degree of certainty* the payoff, not the answer itself.

**Evidence tags**, applied to each theory:
- **CONFIRMED** — directly documented by primary evidence
- **PROBABLE** — strongly supported but not directly proven
- **POSSIBLE** — consistent with known evidence but speculative
- **UNSUPPORTED** — no meaningful evidence
- **DISPROVEN** — contradicted by available evidence

**Case status**, the closing verdict:
- **SOLVED** — evidence overwhelmingly points to one explanation
- **PROBABLE** — strongest explanation identified, but not provable (attach a rough confidence level, e.g. "~70%")
- **UNSOLVED** — insufficient evidence to distinguish between leading theories

"Case Status: Unsolved" becomes a branded, credible moment instead of a letdown — viewers start watching *for* the case status the way true-crime audiences watch for the reveal.

### The evidence board — functional, not decorative

The dossier/evidence-board visual treatment should track the actual case state on screen, not just look moody:

- **KNOWN** panel: last confirmed position, weather, cargo, crew, radio communication, official investigation
- **UNKNOWN** panel: exact final position, cause, mechanical condition
- When a theory is introduced, the board updates live: `+ supporting evidence` / `? open questions` / `– contradicting evidence`

This gives viewers the feeling of solving the case alongside the narrator — a much stronger hook than cinematic B-roll, and genuinely harder for a copycat to replicate, because it's tied to the research methodology itself, not just an art style.

### Recurring segment: "What we know vs. what documentaries claim"

A repeatable mechanism for handling inflated/mythologized details without becoming a pure debunking channel:

> **POPULAR STORY:** "The crew were found dead, frozen in terror."
> **DOCUMENTED:** No surviving primary source confirms this.
> **STATUS:** Unverified.

This also turns the Tier 4 sourcing problem (see Section 6) into content instead of something to quietly avoid.

## 5. Episode sequencing

The first three episodes should prove three different things about the channel, not just hit the top three keywords in order:

1. **USS Cyclops — "this format works."** Best keyword economics (opportunity 70/100, competition 8.5), fully unsolved, clean first test of the evidence-rating system.
2. **MV Joyita — "this channel can handle a complicated story."** Strong existing proof point (a related video already broke out on a 1,090-subscriber channel), real primary-source anchor (the Fiji Royal Commission inquiry).
3. **Ourang Medan — "this channel investigates legends, not just ships."** First "claim → source check" episode. Establishes the second content category from day one, and handle the contested/hoax-adjacent sourcing by inviting the audience into the fact-check rather than lecturing them.

**Acquisition episodes vs. identity episodes:** not every video needs to do both jobs. High-search-volume cases (Cyclops, Joyita) bring new viewers in via search. Lower-search but visually strong cases (Baychimo — "the ship that wouldn't die") work once the audience already trusts the channel and clicks because of who's telling the story, not what they searched. Sequence Baychimo-type episodes after the channel identity is established, not before.

---

## 6. Sourcing discipline (this is the actual moat)

Maintain an internal source hierarchy:

- **Tier 1 — Primary:** government investigations, naval records, court records, contemporary logs
- **Tier 2 — Scholarly:** academic books, papers, archival research
- **Tier 3 — Reputable secondary:** established historians, museums, maritime organizations
- **Tier 4 — Internet lore:** blogs, forums, copied mystery-site articles

Tier 4 material gets investigated on camera, never cited as fact — this is where the "claim → source check" segment lives. Being upfront that a detail traces back to nothing more than a repeated blog post is a credibility builder, not a weakness to hide.

---

## 7. Narrator and voice

This is a load-bearing decision and needs its own pass before recording episode one — it was underdeveloped in the original concept.

**The register:** calm, skeptical, precise. A forensic investigator working through a problem, not a documentary narrator building suspense and not a channel "personality."

- Avoid: *"This terrifying mystery has baffled everyone!"* / *"Hello guys, I'm Captain Mystery..."*
- Aim for: *"There's a problem with that theory. The surviving records contradict it."* / *"At 10:30 p.m., the ship sent its last known transmission."*

If using TTS, avoid the generic "documentary AI voice" cadence — the subject is historical investigation, and the voice needs to convey that someone actually did the research, which a slightly restrained, less polished delivery often communicates better than an ultra-smooth synthetic narrator.

---

## 8. Growth strategy

- **Bridge search to browse in titles.** Keyword-clean titles ("USS Cyclops Disappearance") are SEO-correct but browse-inert — they mean nothing to someone who doesn't already know the ship. Pair the known entity with the unresolved question: *"USS Cyclops — 309 People Vanished. What Happened?"*
- **Vary the closing engagement prompt.** "Which theory do you buy?" goes stale by episode five and can undercut analytical authority when the evidence clearly favors one theory. Rotate: *"What did we miss?"* / *"What piece of evidence would change your mind?"* / *"What question should the original inquiry have asked?"*
- **Clip verdict/reveal moments for Shorts** once episodes are live — a strong case-status reveal is a natural short-form hook back to the full video.
- **Re-check saturation periodically, not per-video.** The one real adjacent competitor (The Microcosm) is growing ~70% YoY — the space is warming up, which argues for establishing the evidence-rating/case-file format clearly in the first few episodes rather than drifting into it slowly.

---

## 9. What to avoid

- Generic AI-voice-over-stock-footage with no visual/methodological identity — the exact pattern flagged in 2026 monetization sweeps.
- An identical beat-for-beat experience every episode — vary the investigative mechanic even while keeping the grammar fixed.
- Playing hoax-adjacent legends completely straight, or conversely, lecturing the audience about being fooled — invite them into the fact-check instead.
- Leading with low-search-demand cases before the channel has identity-driven viewers.
- Copyrighted documentary/news footage — stick to public-domain archives plus original reconstructions.
- Overclaiming the word "investigation" — never imply new evidence, new witnesses, or forensic work that isn't actually happening.
- Committing to an upload cadence before timing the first 2-3 episodes honestly.

## 10. What to do

- Build the fixed-grammar template once (hook → case → timeline → theories → evidence → verdict) and apply the evidence-rating system from episode one.
- Make the evidence board functional — it should visibly update as theories are introduced, not just look atmospheric.
- Cite real Tier 1/2 sources on screen where they exist.
- Keep the narrator register consistent across episodes: forensic, restrained, precise.
- Treat the first three episodes as a thesis proof, then let the format evolve based on actual retention, comments, and rewatched moments before locking anything further.
- Expand the brand framing to "maritime investigations" early, so the ship-cases/legend-cases taxonomy has room to grow into wrecks, hoaxes, WWII mysteries, and engineering failures later.
